Before ’48

The Negev Was Not Empty

These documents were not written by Bedouin. They were written by the Ottomans, the British and the Zionist institutions, and they describe a Negev that was cultivated, settled and owned: fields, houses, sale deeds and tax payments

This page is based on the research of Oren Yiftachel, Atawa Abu Freih, Ahmad Amara, Sandy Kedar and others

Background: divided, cultivated fields in the Beersheba Valley, 1945. Aerial photograph from the Mandate period

In court, the state argues that the Negev was “dead land”: desolate, without farming or settlement, with Bedouin wandering through it until the 1940s.

You can check this without relying on a single Bedouin. All it takes is reading who wrote what, and when.

The Zionist movement

The Surveyors and the Buyers

The Zionist survey, the consultations on buying land, and the estimates from the Zionist side

1920The first Zionist survey

The Zionists Survey the Negev: 92 Tribes

The Palestine Land Development Company (Hakhsharat HaYishuv), a Zionist land-purchasing body headed by Dr. Jacob Thon, published in 1920 the first comprehensive survey of the Negev. The survey documented 92 tribes, their land holdings, their cultivated areas and their patterns of residence.

According to the summary figures, the survey credited the Bedouin with ownership of about 2.66 million dunams in the Beersheba area, about 35% of it cultivated
Zionist bodyPalestine Land Development Company survey, 1920
Mainly tillers of the soil, on the way to becoming fellahin. They live on their land and leave it only for grazing.
Zionist bodyOn the Tiyaha tribes, from the Palestine Land Development Company survey, 1920 (translated from English)

An example: the al-Uqbi tribe

The survey lists the tribe “Arab Aqbana,” headed by Sheikh Muhammad al-Uqbi: 100 houses (including tents), about 26,000 dunams, about 10,000 of them cultivated with wheat, durra and barley, and two water wells.

1925–1948Jewish land purchases

The Buyers Knew Who the Owners Were

In 1925 senior figures of Zionist settlement met to discuss buying land in the Negev. Asked by Yehoshua Hankin, one of the movement’s leading land buyers, who owned the land, he answered in one line.

Tribes. But you have to buy from each and every one, you need a title deed from each and every holder of the land.
Zionist bodyYehoshua Hankin, at a meeting chaired by Menachem Ussishkin, head of the Jewish National Fund, 1925 (translated from English)

By the end of the Mandate, more than 100,000 dunams were bought from Bedouin in deals with Jewish buyers, private and institutional. About 65,000 dunams were registered in the name of the JNF. A large part of the land was used to found Revivim, Hatzerim, Nir Yitzhak, Dorot, Negba, Be’eri, Mishmar HaNegev, Shoval and Ruhama.

In 1926 Mishmar HaNegev bought land near al-Araqib, and the deal was recognized.

The residents of al-Araqib, who had bought land from the al-Uqbi family a few years earlier, were defined as trespassers, and their homes are demolished again and again.

1939A Zionist estimate

Millions of Cultivated Dunams

In 1939 Eliyahu Epstein estimated that between 2.1 and 3.5 million dunams were cultivated in the northern Negev.

The Ottomans

Until 1917

A census, a decision and deeds from the rule that preceded the British

1596The Ottoman census

More Than a Hundred Bedouin Cultivated Areas

The Ottoman tax census of 1596 recorded more than a hundred cultivated areas, “mazra’at,” in the southern Hebron hills, in an area that partly overlaps the northern Negev. More than four hundred years ago, there were already Bedouin cultivating land there

1863–1883British explorers passing through

Those Who Passed Through Saw Fields

Explorers who crossed the Beersheba area in the 19th century described it without meaning to give legal testimony

One marked sign of the Beersheba region as the border between the desert and the hill country: the growing of grain by the Arabs in large, unfenced plots. This is the first place where we found cultivated land.
British explorerHenry Tristram, 1863–1864
The extent of cultivated land here, as well as all the way to Gaza, is immense, and includes crops such as wheat, barley and maize, far exceeding the needs of the inhabitants.
British geologistEdward Hull, on the road from Beersheba to Gaza, 1883

Not everyone saw fields. Some travelers, such as Edward Palmer, described a wasteland, but their routes ran east of Beersheba, in more arid areas, where the Bedouin did indeed live at lower density.

1891The Ottoman Council of State

The Empire Records 5 Million Dunams

On 4 May 1891 the Ottoman Council of State recorded long-standing Bedouin possession of about 5 million Ottoman dunams in the Gaza district.

The Council did not propose to expropriate them. It proposed a mechanism for measuring and registering the lands, with the participation of Bedouin representatives.

The authorities knew this was land with owners. Their question was how to register it.

Early 20th centuryThe founding of Beersheba

The Empire Buys Beersheba

When the Ottomans founded Beersheba, they did not expropriate the land. They bought about 2,000 dunams from the Azazma tribes. The purchase deed specifies the boundaries of the plot and the names of the rights-holders.

An empire does not buy land that has no owner.

Deals were also made among the Bedouin themselves, by deeds, “sanads,” signed before witnesses and specifying boundaries and owners. In the picture: a sale deed from al-Araqib dated 1911.

The British

1917–1948

A promise, a committee, a census and a reply to the Agency

29 March 1921Jerusalem

Churchill Promises the Sheikhs

Reaffirmed the assurances given in Beersheba by the High Commissioner to the sheikhs, that the special rights and customs of the Bedouin tribes of Beersheba will not be interfered with.

…the special rights and customs of the Bedouin Tribes of Beersheba will not be interfered with.

British ruleWinston Churchill, Colonial Secretary, at a meeting with sheikhs from the Beersheba district
July 1921The Abramson Commission

A British Commission Finds 2.8 Million Dunams Under Bedouin Cultivation

The Abramson Commission, set up by the British High Commissioner to determine which lands could be registered as state land, found about 2.8 million dunams in Bedouin ownership and cultivation in the Beersheba area.

1931–1945The British count and map

British Rule Knew Too

In the 1931 census, 89.3% of the Bedouin surveyed in the Beersheba area gave agriculture as their main livelihood. In 1937 the Jewish Agency, headed by Ben-Gurion, approached the Mandate government and asked to settle the Negev on the claim that most of the land there is “mawat,” desolate. The reply:

The cultivable land in the Beersheba sub-district is regarded as belonging to the Bedouin tribes by virtue of possession from time immemorial.

The cultivable land in the Beersheba sub-district is regarded as belonging to the Bedouin tribes by virtue of possession from time immemorial.

British ruleMandate government, in reply to the Jewish Agency, 1937

A British map of 1945, produced from aerial photographs, marks clusters of tents and buildings across the northern Negev. Cross-checking with other sources shows that the large clusters usually also had stone or mud houses and agricultural storage buildings.

The State of Israel

1948 onward

What the state’s own officials wrote, and what was actually done

1951Southern Command

Dayan: The Transfer Cancels Ownership

On 25 September 1951 the Southern Command chief, Moshe Dayan, wrote a letter on moving the Bedouin from the Shoval area to Tel Arad. In clause 4 he explained what the transfer does to their rights:

Moving the Bedouin to new areas will cancel their right as owners of the land, and they will be tenants of government land.
The State of IsraelMoshe Dayan, Southern Command chief, 25 September 1951, in a letter on moving the Bedouin from the Shoval area to Tel Arad

October 1952

The State Already Knew

“It is a known fact that during the Mandate, very large areas were registered in the names of the Bedouin, on the basis of proof that they had cultivated these areas throughout the prescription period, and an important part of these lands was transferred, after their registration, to the Keren Kayemet, to other Jewish companies and to private Jews. So in this matter there are hundreds of precedents, and we believe that the Government of Israel cannot, and need not, ignore them.”
The State of IsraelThe Justice Ministry’s three-member committee: Yosef Weitz, a senior figure at the Jewish National Fund, Yehoshua Palmon, the Prime Minister’s adviser on Arab affairs, and Binyamin Fishman, of the Land Registration and Settlement Department

The committee recommended that the state recognize ownership rights in areas where the Bedouin remaining in Israel could prove they had cultivated throughout the prescription period.

What Was Done With It After 1948

  • 1948According to researchers, about 80% of the Bedouin residents of the Beersheba district fled or were expelled, and were prevented from returning. In the early 1950s the military government concentrated many of those who remained in the Siyag area.
  • 1951The military governor ordered the members of the al-Uqbi tribe to evacuate al-Araqib. The transfer was presented as temporary, and the tribe asked to return. In 1954 the state expropriated the land.
  • 1971–1979In the land settlement process in the northern Negev, 3,220 ownership claims were filed. Their adjudication was in effect frozen for decades.
  • 2015The Supreme Court rejected the al-Uqbi family’s appeal over six plots in the al-Araqib area. It saw Churchill’s promise as a general and vague statement, and distinguished between the registration of sale deals during the Mandate and proof of ownership of land not yet settled.
